\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We predict, for every driver in every Formula~1 race from 1950 to 2024, whether the driver finishes on the podium (\code{positionOrder} $\le 3$), using only information available after qualifying and before the start.
The raw relational tables are audited and cleaned on copies (160{,}076 \verb|\N| sentinels converted, 85 duplicated driver-race pairs resolved, keys verified), three data-engineering questions are answered with multi-hop joins, and a leakage-safe modeling table of 24{,}645 starts is built.
Feature groups are selected by adding one group at a time on a season-based validation split, which keeps the starting grid and the driver's championship standings after the previous round.
A logistic regression, a random forest and a shallow neural network all beat a grid-only baseline: test PR-AUC 0.708, 0.721 and 0.704 against 0.647.
Permutation importance, SHAP and LIME show that the models rely mostly on the starting grid; these explanations describe the models, not causes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Task and dataset}

\paragraph{Task.} The unit of analysis is one driver entering one race, identified by \code{(raceId, driverId)}. The target is binary: \code{podium = 1} if \code{positionOrder} $\le 3$, otherwise 0.
The prediction is made \textbf{after qualifying and before the race starts}. Anything that is only known during or after the race (finishing position, points, laps, race time, fastest lap, retirement status, standings after the race) is \emph{leakage} and is never used as an input.

\paragraph{Dataset.} The Kaggle/Ergast \textit{Formula 1 World Championship (1950--2024)} dataset~\cite{f1dataset}: 14 relational CSV tables.
\code{results} is the central table (one row per driver per race); its ID columns point to \code{races}, \code{drivers}, \code{constructors} (teams) and \code{status}, and \code{races} points to \code{circuits}.
The raw files are never modified: every cleaning step works on in-memory copies.

\paragraph{Tools.} pandas, numpy, matplotlib, seaborn and missingno for data handling and plots; scikit-learn~\cite{sklearn} for the statistical models; TensorFlow/Keras for the neural network; shap and lime for the explanations.
The notebook runs top to bottom with a single \textit{Run All}, on Kaggle or locally.

Each table was loaded with a plain \code{pd.read\_csv}, letting pandas infer the column types.
A standard first inspection (\code{head}, \code{info}, \code{describe}) of \code{results} immediately exposed the main data-quality issue: \code{position}, \code{milliseconds}, \code{fastestLap}, \code{rank} and \code{fastestLapSpeed} contain numbers but were read as text.

\paragraph{Findings that drive later decisions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item A missing finishing \code{position} means the driver did not finish. This is information, not a gap, so it is never imputed.
\item Qualifying times are missing for most of the history (\textbf{qualifying-coverage drift}). They therefore cannot be a feature over 1950--2024; the starting grid, which exists for every race, is used instead.
\item \code{grid = 0} occurs 1{,}638 times and mixes two cases: 72 drivers who started from the pit lane (laps $>0$) and drivers who never started (did not qualify or withdrew, 0 laps) (\fig{fig:grid}).
\item Before the 1990s many more drivers entered a race than could start, inflating the number of entries per race.
\item The same value is spelled in different ways: \code{USA} / \code{United States} in \code{circuits}, and \code{Argentine} / \code{'Argentinian '} (with a trailing space), \code{East German} and double nationalities in \code{drivers}. These were found with \code{value\_counts()}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Key checks and the one-row-per-driver-per-race rule}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Primary keys.} All 14 primary keys (single columns, or \code{raceId+driverId+lap} for \code{lap\_times} and \code{raceId+driverId+stop} for \code{pit\_stops}) are unique and never empty.
\item \textbf{Foreign keys.} All 22 foreign-key relations reconcile: every \code{raceId}, \code{driverId}, \code{constructorId}, \code{statusId} and \code{circuitId} exists in the table it points to (0 orphans). The notebook stops with an assertion if either check fails.
\item \textbf{Duplicates.} 85 \code{(raceId, driverId)} pairs appear more than once (176 rows), all between 1950 and 1978. These are \emph{shared drives}: a driver took over a teammate's car during the race.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Duplicate rule.} We keep the row of the car the driver \textbf{started} in, meaning the row with the lowest real grid slot. Which car a driver starts in is known before the race; how each car finishes is not, so this rule matches the pre-race view of the task. If neither row has a grid slot, the driver never started; the row with the better finish is kept, and the row is excluded from modeling anyway. The alternative rule (keep the best finish) would change only 17 podium labels, all before 1979 (decision log, D3).

\section{Target, pre-race cutoff and metrics}

Only drivers who started are counted: 13.5\% of starts end on the podium (\fig{fig:target}), so the classes are imbalanced. This is why PR-AUC, not accuracy, is the primary metric (see below).
The rate follows $3/\text{(starters per race)}$, so it is lower in the large-field 1950s--80s ($\approx$12\%) than today ($\approx$15\%); this era shift is one reason the data is split by season (Section~\ref{sec:split}).
The only gaps between the two lines are 8 races in 1951--1957 with shared drives: either a podium car is credited to several drivers, or the dropped duplicate row (Section~2.3) held the podium.

\label{fig:target}
\end{figure}

\begin{table}[H]

\caption{The pre-race cutoff.}
\label{tab:cutoff}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{XX}
\toprule
Allowed (known before the start) & Not allowed (only known after the race) \\
\midrule
\code{grid} (qualifying result after penalties) & \code{position}, \code{positionText}, \code{positionOrder}, \code{points} \\

Sprint results are not used: sprints exist only since 2021.

\paragraph{Metric choice.} With 13.5\% positives, a model that always predicts ``no podium'' is about 85\% accurate (0.866 on train, 0.850 on validation, 0.849 on test) and useless, so accuracy is reported for reference only and an ``always no podium'' row is shown next to the results.
The \textbf{primary metric is PR-AUC} (average precision): it measures how well the real podium finishers are ranked above everyone else, and it focuses on the rare class.
The \textbf{secondary metrics} are ROC-AUC (overall separation of the two classes) and F1 at a decision threshold chosen on validation (the quality of the actual yes/no decision). Precision and recall are reported alongside F1.

\section{Data-engineering questions}

Each answer joins several tables. After every join the notebook asserts that the number of rows did not change, so no join duplicated rows. Rates are given with 95\% Wilson intervals~\cite{wilson1927}, which stay sensible for small samples.

\subsection{Q1: Which circuits convert a front-row start into a podium most often, and did that change after 2014?}

\paragraph{Method.} Join \code{results} $\rightarrow$ \code{races} $\rightarrow$ \code{circuits}.
\textbf{Grain:} one front-row starter (grid slot 1 or 2) in one race.
\textbf{Qualifying vs.\ grid:} the two can differ because of grid penalties. We use \code{grid}, the slot the car actually started from: the podium is fought from there, and grid exists for every race since 1950, whereas qualifying positions start in 1994.
\textbf{Missingness:} \code{grid} and \code{positionOrder} have no missing values.
\textbf{Cutoff:} the front-row condition is known before the start; the podium outcome is what is being measured.
Non-starters and the Indianapolis~500 are excluded, as in the modeling table. We only rank a circuit if it has hosted at least 5 races in each era (before 2014 and from 2014). Fewer races than that make the percentage meaningless: a circuit that held one race has only two front-row cars, so its score can only be 0\%, 50\% or 100\%, which is pure luck. \tab{tab:q1funnel} shows how the 77 circuits come down to the 16 that can be ranked: most were raced in only one era, so they cannot show whether their ranking changed.

\paragraph{Answer.} A front-row start converts much more often in the hybrid era: \textbf{55.9\% before 2014} (994 of 1{,}778; 95\% CI 53.6--58.2\%) \textbf{vs.\ 76.0\% from 2014} (345 of 454; 71.9--79.7\%).
The best circuits since 2014 are Suzuka (89\%), Yas Marina (86\%) and Monaco (85\%); the weakest of the ranked circuits are the Hungaroring (59\%) and Mexico City (61\%).
The two eras' rankings show \textbf{no detectable agreement}: the Spearman rank correlation is $-0.03$ over 16 circuits, and when the circuits are redrawn it could lie anywhere between about $-0.64$ and $+0.55$. Monaco, for example, moved from 15th of 16 before 2014 (49\%) to 3rd (85\%). The data do not show that a track that rewards a front-row start in one era does so in the other.

\subsection{Q2: Do drivers podium more often at home, controlling for starting position?}

\paragraph{Method.} Join \code{results} $\rightarrow$ \code{drivers} $\rightarrow$ \code{races} $\rightarrow$ \code{circuits}. A home race is a race in the driver's \code{home\_country} (cleaning step~3).
\textbf{Grain:} one start (non-starters and the Indianapolis~500 excluded): 24{,}645 starts, 1{,}934 of them at home (7.8\%).
\textbf{Missingness:} every driver has a home country (asserted).
\textbf{Cutoff:} starting position and home status are known before the start.
To control for starting position, podium rates are compared \emph{within} starting-position groups (pole, 2--3, 4--6, 7--10, 11+), and the home-minus-away gaps are averaged, weighted by home starts. A logistic regression of podium on home, starting group and era gives the home effect as an odds ratio.

\section{Modeling table and data split}\label{sec:split}

The modeling table has one row per driver who \emph{started} a race in 1950--2024, without the Indianapolis~500: \textbf{24{,}645 rows}, with \code{(raceId, driverId)} unique (asserted). Pit-lane starters are placed one slot behind the last car.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering\small
\caption{Season-based split.}
\label{tab:split}
\begin{tabular}{llrrrl}
\toprule
Part & Seasons & Rows & Podiums & Rate & Used for \\
\midrule
Train & 1950--2017 & 21{,}672 & 2{,}895 & 13.4\% & fitting models, imputer, scaler \\
Validation & 2018--2021 & 1{,}619 & 243 & 15.0\% & features, settings, thresholds \\
Test & 2022--2024 & 1{,}354 & 204 & 15.1\% & final scores, used once \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Why split by season.} The task is to predict future races. A random split (\code{train\_test\_split}) would put drivers from the same race on both sides, and the model would be trained on seasons later than the ones it is tested on. Both make scores look better than they are. Cutting by season mimics real use. The validation block (4 seasons, 243 podiums) is large enough to compare models, and the test block covers the newest regulations (2022--2024).

\subsection{Candidate features and their validity at the cutoff}

Points are used as a share of the leader's points so that seasons with different points systems are comparable.
Driver and team identity enter only through these features. One-hot encoding the $\approx$900 driver IDs would add hundreds of sparse columns, and many test drivers never appear in training.
\textbf{Leakage check:} the notebook recomputes recent form for 150 random rows by hand, from earlier races only, and asserts that the values match. Standings are joined on the previous round's \code{raceId}, with a check that the join adds no rows.

\section{Preprocessing}\label{sec:prep}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Imputation:} missing values (round~1 standings, rookies) are filled with the \textbf{median} of the training rows (\code{SimpleImputer}). The median is robust to skewed columns such as wins. Fill values: grid 12, \code{drv\_pos} 12, \code{drv\_pts\_share} 0.077, \code{drv\_wins} 0.
\item \textbf{Scaling:} \code{StandardScaler} gives each feature mean 0 and standard deviation 1, so large-valued features (grid 1--34, positions up to 102) do not dominate small ones (shares 0--1). Scaling matters for logistic regression and the neural network; the forest is unaffected, but every model receives identical inputs so the comparison is fair.
\end{enumerate}
Both steps are fit on the \textbf{training seasons only} and then applied unchanged to validation and test. \fig{fig:scaling} shows the effect: scaling shifts and stretches the values but keeps the shape of the distribution (for example the spike at \code{drv\_pts\_share} = 0, when drivers have no points yet). Training data ends up with mean 0 and std 1; test does not (means between $-0.27$ and $+0.23$), which confirms the scaler was not refit on it.

\section{Models}

All models use the same rows and the same four inputs, so their scores are directly comparable.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Baseline: logistic regression on \code{grid} only.} The simplest reasonable model. Any other model must beat it to justify its extra complexity.
\item \textbf{Logistic regression:} a weighted sum of the inputs passed through a sigmoid, giving a probability. It is fast and interpretable, but it can only learn effects that keep going in one direction.
\item \textbf{Random forest}~\cite{breiman2001}\textbf{:} 300 decision trees, each trained on a random sample of rows and features; their votes are averaged. It learns curved effects and interactions, but it is harder to interpret and its probabilities are coarse.
\item \textbf{Shallow neural network} (Keras): one or two hidden \code{Dense} ReLU layers and a single sigmoid output neuron, trained with binary cross-entropy and Adam. Early stopping on validation loss (patience 15, best weights restored) guards against overfitting. It is flexible but a black box, and it depends on scaling and random initialisation.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Tuning (validation only).} Each combination of class weighting (none / \code{balanced}) and model settings was trained, and the one with the best validation PR-AUC was kept (\tab{tab:tuning}). The decision threshold of each model is the one that maximises F1 on validation: baseline 0.428, logistic regression 0.400, random forest 0.437, network 0.337.

\section{Explainability (XAI)}

\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.92\linewidth}{\textbf{Explanations are not causal effects.} A feature being important to a model means the model \emph{relies on it to make predictions}. It is not evidence that the feature \emph{causes} podium finishes. For example, a front grid slot is mostly a sign of a fast car, and the fast car is what produces podiums. Correlated inputs (the three standings columns) share the credit, and how they share it depends on the method.}}
\end{center}

\section{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Qualifying-coverage drift.} Qualifying times exist only from 1994, Q2/Q3 from 2003, and only for the faster drivers. The model uses the grid (the qualifying result after penalties) and no qualifying times, so information such as the gap to pole is not used.
\item \textbf{Era sensitivity.} Field sizes, points systems and reliability changed greatly over 75 years. Points are normalised by the leader's points and the split is by season, but training rows from the 1950s still differ strongly from 2022--2024.
\item \textbf{Dominant teams.} The validation and test seasons are each dominated by one team (Mercedes 2018--2021, Red Bull 2022--2024), so the scores and explanations describe those eras.
\item \textbf{Race-day events are invisible.} Rain, crashes, safety cars and mechanical failures are not known before the start; the Kvyat example shows the effect.
\item \textbf{Round~1 and rookies use training medians.} About 5\% of validation and test rows have no standings and receive a typical mid-table value. In a side check, using last season's final standings instead helped validation (+0.007 to +0.011 PR-AUC) but not clearly test, so the simple median was kept.
\item \textbf{Feature selection depends on the order of the groups.} The part that is stable is the driver standings on top of the grid; the exact list of extra features is not (Section on feature selection).
\item \textbf{Small validation set.} With 243 validation podiums, differences of about 0.01 PR-AUC between models are within noise; the model ranking is not stable between validation and test.
\item \textbf{Explanations are not causal} and describe one model on 2018--2021 rows.
\item \textbf{Q1--Q3} rely on the dataset's status labels and on a simplified nationality-to-country mapping. Mechanical rates include unclear retirements, and the Q2 home definition ignores drivers who live abroad.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
After auditing and cleaning the 14 tables, a leakage-safe table of 24{,}645 starts was built and split by season.
The data-engineering questions show that front-row starts convert to podiums far more often since 2014 (55.9\% $\rightarrow$ 76.0\%) with no detectable agreement between the circuit rankings of the two eras, that there is no home advantage once starting position is controlled for, and that mechanical retirements fell from 11.2\% to 6.9\% of starts.
For prediction, the starting grid plus the driver's championship standings before the race are enough: logistic regression, a random forest and a shallow neural network all clearly beat the grid-only baseline (test PR-AUC 0.70--0.72 vs.\ 0.647) and perform about the same.
Ablation and XAI agree that the grid carries most of the signal, with the season standings adding a consistent but smaller contribution.
